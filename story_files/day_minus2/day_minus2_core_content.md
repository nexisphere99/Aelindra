## SCENE 1: EDRIN'S CHAMBERS   MORNING
### [Passage: `day-2_wake`]

The ceiling hasn't changed.

I've been staring at it long enough to know   same crack running from the northwest corner toward the chandelier mount, same smoke-stain halo around the iron fixture where the candles burn too close to the stone. I've mapped this ceiling since I was twelve years old and couldn't sleep because Mother was coughing in the east wing and I could hear it through two walls and a courtyard.

She's coughing now. Not that I can hear it from here. I just know. The way you know rain is coming when the air goes heavy and the birds stop singing.

Grey light pushes through the arrow-slit windows. Dawn in Aethermere is never generous   it doesn't flood in, doesn't announce itself with warmth. It seeps. A slow invasion of pale nothing that turns everything the colour of old iron.

I swing my legs off the bed. Stone floor. Cold enough to make me hiss through my teeth. Someone let the fire die   no, I told the servants not to tend it overnight. I don't like people in my rooms while I sleep. A prince's paranoia, Aldric calls it. A reasonable precaution, I call it. Not like anyone's tried to kill me. Yet.

My chambers are... modest, for a prince. That's what the courtiers whisper, anyway, in that tone that makes "modest" sound like a disease. Bookshelves line two walls   histories, treatises on statecraft, a worn collection of Thornridge campaign journals that belonged to my grandmother. A training sword hangs above the desk, blade dull but grip worn smooth from years of evening drills when the yard was occupied. Maps pinned to the wall   the kingdom, the neighbouring territories, a crude sketch of the Ashborne lands I drew myself when I was seventeen and furious about something I can't remember now.

The half-finished letter on the desk catches my eye. I started writing to Rowan three weeks ago. Got as far as *"The court is suffocating and I'm considering taking up goat herding as an alternative career"* before I lost the energy to be funny. The ink has dried in the nib.

I dress. Linen shirt, leather jerkin   the good one, with the reinforced shoulders, because I'm sparring this morning and last time I wore the thin one Aldric's practice blade left a bruise the shape of a crescent moon on my collarbone. Dark trousers. Boots. The boots are getting worn at the heels. I should have the cobbler replace them. I won't. I've broken them in perfectly and new boots are a betrayal of the feet.

The mirror.

I don't know why I stop in front of it today. I pass it every morning   it's bolted to the wall beside the wardrobe, a sheet of polished silver in a dark wood frame. Most days I glance. Today I look.

A lean man stares back. Dark hair falling over grey-blue eyes that my mother calls "storm-coloured," which is a kind way of saying they can't decide what they are. Sharp jaw, a little too sharp   I haven't been eating enough, and it shows in the hollows beneath my cheekbones. Shadows under my eyes because I slept four hours, maybe five. Handsome, supposedly. Rosalind Thorne told me once I had "the face of a man who doesn't know he's beautiful, which is either charming or infuriating depending on my mood." I didn't know what to do with that then. Still don't.

My shoulders are broad from six years of swordwork. Arms ropey rather than bulky   I'm fast, not strong, and Aldric has trained me accordingly. I run my hand through my hair. It flops back immediately. I don't bother trying again.

This is me. This body. These bones and muscles and the rough scrape of stubble under my jaw that I should shave but won't because there's a petty satisfaction in looking slightly unkempt when the courtiers expect polish.

I don't know why I catalogue it all this morning. Some instinct, maybe. The way you unconsciously memorize a room you're about to leave.

A knock. The door opens   a servant, young, nervous, carrying a tray. Bread, hard cheese, cold venison from last night's roast. A cup of something steaming that smells like it's trying to be tea and failing.

"Your Highness. Breakfast."

"On the table."

He sets it down. Lingers.

"Something else?"

"The Queen's Healer was summoned at first light, Your Highness. Captain Vane asked me to tell you Her Majesty has requested your presence. At your convenience, he said, but..." He falters. "He said it the way he says things that aren't actually at your convenience."

My stomach clenches. I nod. The servant leaves.

I eat standing at the window. The bread is yesterday's   slightly stale, the way I prefer it, because fresh bread is too soft and tears when you try to spread anything on it. Below, the courtyard is busy. Guards changing shift. Stable hands leading horses. A merchant's cart being inspected at the inner gate. Normal. Everything looks so bloody normal.

The cheese tastes like nothing. I eat it anyway. Energy is energy.

## SCENE 2: TRAINING YARD   MORNING DRILL
### [Passage: `day-2_training`]

The training yard smells the way it always smells   iron, leather, sweat, and the particular dusty chalk they spread on the flagstones to keep the footing honest. Morning light slants across the open square, catching the edge of practice blades lined up on the rack like surgical tools.

Aldric is already here. Of course he is. I'm convinced the man sleeps standing up in his armour somewhere in the barracks and simply opens his eyes when the sun touches the horizon. He's running a whetstone along his practice blade   not because it needs sharpening, practice blades are intentionally dull, but because the motion calms him. I've watched him do it before every spar for six years.

He's tall. Taller than me by two inches, which irritates me more than it should. Broad across the shoulders and chest, built like something designed for the specific purpose of breaking other things. A scar runs from his left temple to his jaw   a bandit's knife, three years ago, when he threw himself between me and a blade during a hunting trip. I told him that was unnecessary. He told me it was literally his job. We didn't speak for two days because we were both right.

"Your Highness." He sets down the whetstone. Stands. The motion is economical   no wasted energy, no theatrical flourish. Aldric moves the way a sword cuts: direct.

"Captain."

"You look like hell."

"Thank you, Aldric. Your gift for courtly flattery remains unmatched."

The ghost of a smile. He hands me a practice blade   weighted steel, edge rounded, heavy enough to simulate the real thing without removing limbs. I take it. The grip is familiar. My palm fits the leather wrapping the way a key fits a lock.

We circle. The yard is empty this early   just us and the pigeons on the battlement. The air is cool. My breath comes in thin clouds.

Aldric moves first. A testing strike   high, right side, the kind you can read from across a continent. I parry. Turn it. Riposte. He blocks without looking, which is the most insulting thing a swordsman can do to another swordsman, and steps inside my guard.

My mind isn't in it. My mind is in my mother's chambers, in the copper-sweet smell of medicinal herbs that doesn't quite cover the other smell, the one the healers pretend doesn't exist. The smell of someone dying slowly.

His blade touches my ribs. I didn't see the feint.

"Dead," he says, stepping back.

"Best of three."

We reset. I'm faster this time   anger is a decent substitute for focus, in short bursts. I catch his blade on the cross-guard, twist, drive forward. For half a second I see something in his eyes   surprise, maybe approval. Then he drops his shoulder, pivots, and the flat of his practice sword cracks across my forearm hard enough to numb my fingers. My blade clatters to the flagstones.

"Your mind isn't here, Your Highness."

"My mind is in my mother's lungs, where all the air seems to have gone."

He doesn't say anything for a moment. Aldric isn't good with words   he's good with silences, which is rarer and more useful. He picks up my blade. Hands it back, hilt-first.

"She asked for you. This morning. The Royal Healer was summoned at dawn."

So the servant wasn't exaggerating the urgency. Wonderful.

"I know."

"Go to her."

"I was planning to."

"Go now. The swords will be here tomorrow."

I sheathe the practice blade without cleaning it   muscle memory, not disrespect, but I see Aldric's jaw tighten at the breach of discipline. Any other day he'd correct me. Today, he says nothing. He just watches me cross the yard toward the palace entrance, and when I glance back, he's standing exactly where I left him, hands clasped behind his back, face unreadable.

The pigeons scatter as I pass. Somewhere in the kitchens, someone drops a pot. The morning goes on.

## SCENE 3: QUEEN'S CHAMBERS   THE TRUTH
### [Passage: `day-2_queen`]

The corridor to the east wing is long and gets longer every time I walk it. Portraits line the walls   my grandmother in battle armour, her mother in a coronation gown, her mother's mother on horseback with a falcon on her wrist. Every painting is a woman. Every queen, an unbroken line stretching back six hundred years to the Founding.

And at the end of that line: me. A man. The crack in the chain.

More guards than usual flank the oak door to Mother's chambers. They bow. I nod. One of them   a young soldier I don't know, barely old enough to shave   looks at me with something that might be pity. I hate it immediately.

The door is heavy. Carved with the royal crest: a silver stag beneath a crescent moon. I push it open.

Inside: dim. The curtains are drawn against the daylight, which means it's a bad day. Mother only blocks the sun when the light hurts her eyes, and the light only hurts her eyes when the illness is flaring and the healers have given her the strong tinctures that make her pupils wide and fragile.

The smell hits me. Medicinal herbs   rosemary, meadowsweet, something sharp and astringent I don't know the name of. Honey, because the healers mix it into everything to mask the taste of the bitter draughts and beneath all of it, quiet as a secret, the copper-sweet rot. The smell of her body consuming itself from the inside.

She sits propped against a fortress of pillows, a writing desk balanced across her lap. Papers everywhere   she's been working. Of course she has. Queen Seraphina does not lie in bed and wait to die. She lies in bed and governs, and woe to the minister who assumes her horizontal position indicates diminished authority.

"Close the door. Sit. Don't argue until I finish."

Her voice. Even now, even thinned by illness and the draughts that make her tongue slow, her voice has the quality of a drawn bowstring   controlled tension, the promise of release. I grew up obeying that voice. I sit.

She studies me. Those eyes   grey-blue, same as mine, the one thing the portraits all share across six centuries of queens. Hers are sunken now, ringed dark, but the fire in them could smelt iron.

"How much do you know about the succession law?"

"Maternal line. Eldest daughter inherits. Unbroken for "

"Six hundred and twelve years and do you know why the Elder Council has refused every attempt to alter it?"

"Tradition. Prophecy. Stubbornness."

"The Founding Charter was signed in dragon's blood, Edrin. Whether that's literal or metaphor, it doesn't matter   the Elder Council treats it as divine law. Seven queens have tried to amend it. Seven failed. I was the eighth. I failed three years ago, before the illness, when I still had political capital to burn. The Council said no. They will always say no."

She lifts a document from the desk. Official seal. I recognise the Ashborne crest before she speaks.

"Lord Vareth submitted a formal succession claim last month. I've been blocking the hearing. I can block it for two more days. After that, the Elder Council convenes and rules."

"And they'll rule for Ashborne?"

"They'll rule that without a female heir, the crown passes to the nearest eligible house. Which is Ashborne. Yes."

"Then we fight. We rally the bannermen "

"Vareth has alliances with Tormark, Veldain, and the Free Cities of the Sarrow Coast. Combined forces: twelve thousand professional soldiers. We can field seven thousand, and two thousand of those are garrison troops who haven't fought since the border skirmish nine years ago. The military analysis is not ambiguous, Edrin. If we fight, we lose. Not might lose. Will lose. Within eight weeks."

The room is very quiet. I can hear the candle flames guttering. I can hear my own heartbeat, stupidly loud.

"What about a political marriage? Adopt a girl from another house, marry her to "

"Non-blood heirs have no claim under the Charter. It was tried in the fourth century. The resulting civil war killed twenty thousand people."

"Legitimise a cousin. Father's side "

"Your father was an only child from a minor house with no living relatives. I checked. Twice."

"Change the Elder Council. Replace them with "

"Appointed for life by the previous Council. Self-perpetuating. I can't remove them without cause, and 'they disagree with me' isn't cause."

Every door I open, she's already closed. Every escape route, already bricked shut. She's not presenting a problem   she's presenting a verdict and she knows it. I can see it in the way she watches me: patient, certain, and underneath it all, devastated.

"There is one alternative."

She tells me about the Rite of Becoming.

The Silverveil. The Moonspire Tower. Archsorceress Ilara, who she's already spoken with. The Rite   ancient, forbidden, performed three times in recorded history. Each time, the subject survived. Each time, the transformation was permanent. Complete. Total.

She tells me what it means. What it would do to my body. What I would become.

"In every legal, biological, and magical sense   you would be my daughter. The succession holds. The Ashbornes have no claim. The kingdom survives."

I stare at her. The candles flicker. Somewhere in the palace, a clock chimes noon, and the sound seems to come from very far away, from a world where things make sense and mothers don't ask their sons to 

"You're asking me to stop being your son."

"I'm asking you to save everything I've lived for. Everything your grandmother lived for. Everything your great-grandmother bled for on the battlefield at Thornridge."

"By becoming a *woman*?"

"By becoming the heir this kingdom needs."

The words hang in the air between us. They have weight   I can feel them pressing on my chest, on my ribs, on the lungs that suddenly aren't working the way lungs should work. I'm not breathing. I am, but it doesn't feel like it.

"I need to think."

"You have two days. Then Vareth files his formal challenge, and the Elder Council rules. After that, no Rite, no ritual, no magic in the world will matter. The law will have spoken."

I stand. My legs work, which surprises me. I walk to the door. My hand finds the handle through some miracle of muscle memory because my vision has gone narrow and strange, like looking down a long corridor.

"Edrin."

I stop. Don't turn.

"I didn't do this to you. The world did this. I'm just the one who refuses to pretend it isn't happening."

I leave. I don't slam the door. I want to. I want to put my fist through the ancient oak and feel my knuckles split and bleed because at least that would be a pain I chose, a pain that makes sense, a pain with a clear cause and a clear end.

Instead I press my forehead against the cold stone of the corridor wall. I breathe. In, out. In, out. The stone smells like nothing. Like minerals and centuries and the accumulated indifference of rock that has stood here long before I was born and will stand long after I'm dust.

Two days.

Two days to decide whether I lose my body or my kingdom.

## SCENE 4: GRAND LIBRARY   RESEARCH
### [Passage: `day-2_library`]

The Grand Library of Crown's Rest is three stories of accumulated knowledge, stacked in shelves of dark wood that reach toward vaulted ceilings where painted constellations wheel across plaster skies. Dust motes drift in the slanted afternoon light like slow, aimless snow. The air smells of old paper, binding glue, leather, and the particular dry warmth of a room that has been closed for too long and opened too rarely.

I come here more often than any prince should. That's according to Lord Merrick, the Master of Ceremonies, who believes a prince's time is better spent hunting, jousting, and "projecting vigour"   whatever the hell that means. I come here because books don't bow, don't lie, and don't look at me like I'm a problem to be solved through strategic marriage.

The librarian, Haeth, watches me from his desk near the entrance. He's ancient   seventy, maybe eighty, hunched like a question mark, with eyes sharp enough to read footnotes across the room. He doesn't speak unless spoken to, which makes him the best conversationalist in the palace.

"Haeth. I need everything you have on the Rite of Becoming. The Silverveil's transformation ritual."

One white eyebrow rises. It's the most expressive I've ever seen him.

"Section nine, eastern alcove, top shelf. Three sources   the Chronicle of Fairsworn, the Silverveil Compendium, and an anonymous account bound in blue leather that I've never been able to properly attribute. The succession law codices are in section three if you need those as well."

"I do."

"They haven't changed since the last time you looked. Neither has the Founding Charter."

"I know."

"And yet you're looking again."

I don't answer. He nods, as if my silence told him something, and returns to cataloguing whatever obscure manuscript has claimed his attention today.

I find the books. The eastern alcove is quiet   tucked behind a pillar, lit by a single tall window that faces the inner courtyard. A reading desk, scarred by centuries of scholars' elbows, sits beneath it.

I start with the Chronicle of Fairsworn.

Lord Aldren Fairsworn. Two hundred and eighty years ago. A minor lord from the coastal provinces, last male of a matrilineal line. His mother was dying. His sisters had been killed in a plague. The house would fall without a female heir. Aldren went to the Silverveil. The Rite was performed over seven days. Complete biological transformation. Aldren became Aldra. She bore two daughters in the following decade. Ruled for forty years. Died at seventy-one. The chronicle describes her as "a woman of formidable will who wore her second nature as though it had always been her first."

The language is clinical in the sections describing the transformation itself. *The subject's physical form was reshaped in accordance with the ancient patterns   height diminished, musculature softened, skeletal structure adjusted, reproductive organs replaced in totality.* In totality. I read that phrase four times. Each time it means the same thing and each time I understand it less.

The anonymous account in blue leather is stranger. Less clinical, more   poetic isn't the word. Raw. Written by someone who either experienced the Rite or spoke directly to someone who did:

*On the third day, I woke and my hands were not my hands. Smaller. The knuckles less pronounced. I held them before my face and thought: whose are these? And then: mine. These are mine and the grief was not for what I had lost but for how quickly the new thing began to feel like it had always been there, as if my body was a house and someone had rearranged the furniture and I hated it and also could not deny that the room made more sense this way.*

I close the book. My hands are shaking. I open them on the desk   my hands, my current hands, sword-calloused and large, knuckles scarred from a hundred small injuries in the training yard. I flex my fingers. They respond. They're mine.

For now.

The Silverveil Compendium is drier   magical theory, ritual components, success conditions. The Rite requires the subject's genuine consent ("coercion invalidates the pattern and the magic will not hold"), a lunar alignment that occurs every three months (the next one is in two days   not a coincidence, I realize; Mother planned this), and seven days of sustained ritual during which the subject must remain within the Moonspire Tower.

The succession law codices tell me what I already know: there is no loophole. No precedent for exception. No clever legal argument waiting to be discovered. Seven queens tried. Seven failed. The Charter is absolute.

I sit among the books for a long time. The light shifts   afternoon moving toward evening, the shadows of the shelves lengthening across the floor like dark fingers. At some point a junior librarian comes by with a candle and sets it on the desk without speaking. I don't remember thanking her. I hope I did.

The facts are these: my mother is dying. Without a female heir, the kingdom falls to a man who will strip it to the bone. Every alternative has been tried and has failed. There is one option remaining, and it involves surrendering the body I was born in for one I cannot imagine.

I close the last book. I stack them neatly. Haeth will notice if I don't.

The walk back through the library feels longer than the walk in. The painted constellations on the ceiling turn slowly in the candlelight   an old enchantment, Haeth told me once, cast by a Silverveil sorceress two centuries ago because the Head Librarian at the time liked the idea of stars indoors.

Stars indoors. Magic that rearranges the sky because someone thought it would be pleasant.

Magic that rearranges a body because someone thinks it's necessary.

I push through the library doors into the corridor. The evening air is cooler. Somewhere, someone is playing a lute.

## SCENE 5: ROYAL GARDENS   ROWAN'S ARRIVAL
### [Passage: `day-2_gardens`]

The gardens at dusk are the only place in Crown's Rest that doesn't feel like a cage.

Stone paths wind between flower beds   night-blooming jasmine opening its white throats to the darkening sky, winter roses still holding colour despite the season, herbs planted by some long-dead queen who believed a monarch should know what grew in her own soil. Hedgerows trimmed into geometric shapes border the paths. A fountain at the center   a silver stag pouring water from its antlers   catches the last copper light and scatters it.

I sit on a stone bench near the fountain. The evening air smells of jasmine and woodsmoke from the kitchens. My head is full of books and blood tests and the phrase *in totality* and the look in my mother's eyes when she said *everything your great-grandmother bled for.*

I'm staring at nothing when I hear lute strings.

Not court music   something looser, rougher, with the slightly irregular rhythm of someone playing for themselves rather than an audience. The melody wanders, finds something beautiful, loses it, circles back. It sounds like a conversation between the musician and the instrument, private and unguarded.

I know that style. I'd know it through a wall, through a storm, through ten years of silence.

"Rowan."

They step around the hedgerow, lute slung across their body, half-smile already in place. Rowan Ashwood   half-elf, traveling bard, the closest thing I have to a friend who doesn't call me "Your Highness" and mean it.

They look the same. Different. Both. Their hair is longer   dark with that faint copper undertone that catches light in ways human hair doesn't, swept back from pointed ears and falling past their shoulders. Eyes the colour of honey, tilted slightly, always looking like they know something amusing that they're deciding whether to share. Lean and angular, dressed in travel-worn leather and a shirt that might have been white several washes ago, boots dusty from the road.

We met when I was fifteen. Tournament day. Rowan was busking for coins near the festival stalls, playing a song so filthy that three matrons walked away and two came closer. I laughed so hard I choked on candied apple. We talked for four hours. We've been writing letters   erratically, across distances that shouldn't allow friendship but somehow do   ever since.

"You look like a man deciding which cliff to jump off." They settle onto the bench beside me, adjusting the lute across their lap.

"That's closer to accurate than you think."

"Ah." Rowan doesn't push. That's the thing about them   they have the patience of an immortal, which they're technically halfway to being, and the emotional intelligence of someone who has spent their life reading rooms for a living. They set their fingers on the strings and play something soft. Not a performance. A presence.

"I thought you were in Tormark," I say, because saying anything else would mean saying the real thing, and I'm not ready.

"I was. Terrible audiences. The Tormarkians appreciate a twelve-verse ballad about agricultural policy but look at you blankly if you play something with actual emotion. I left before I died of boredom." They glance at me sideways. "And I got a letter. From your mother, actually. She said you might need a friend."

Of course she did. Of course Mother   even from her sickbed, even while dying, even while planning the demolition and reconstruction of her only child's entire existence   thought to summon the one person who might make the demolition bearable.

"She's thorough."

"She's your mother. It's the same thing."

We sit. Rowan plays. The melody shifts   something older now, a song I half-recognize from childhood. The jasmine exhales its sweetness into the cooling air. Above us, the first stars appear   the real ones, not the painted kind on library ceilings.

"Whatever it is," Rowan says, not looking at me, fingers still moving on the strings, "you've never been a coward, Edrin. You're the bravest person I know. Not because you're fearless, but because you're terrified of everything and do it anyway."

"What if 'doing it' means losing... myself?"

They stop playing. The silence is sudden and specific   the kind that happens when someone puts down an instrument after playing for a long time, and the air hasn't yet figured out how to fill the space.

"I've been remaking myself since I was born." Rowan's voice is quiet. "Half of two worlds, belonging fully to neither. Every town I enter, I decide which version of myself to wear. It's not losing yourself. It's finding out which parts were real all along."

The words land in a place I didn't know was open. Something behind my sternum shifts   not understanding, exactly, but the premonition of understanding. The shadow of a thought I'm not ready to have.

"Stay," I say. "For a few days. I might need..." I don't finish.

"I'm here." Rowan picks up the lute again. "I'm not going anywhere."

They play until the stars are thick and the fountain's splash is the only other sound. I don't tell them about the Rite. Not tonight. Tonight I just need someone beside me who doesn't want anything, who isn't angling for position or advantage, who plays music in the garden because the evening is beautiful and that's enough.

It's not enough. But it helps.

## SCENE 6: EDRIN'S CHAMBERS   NIGHT
### [Passage: `day-2_night`]

My chambers are dark when I return. The fire has been rebuilt   someone came in while I was out, despite my standing orders. I should be annoyed. I'm not. The warmth is welcome.

I undress mechanically. Jerkin on the chair. Shirt over the back. Boots beside the bed, left then right, the way I've done since I was old enough to dress myself. The routine is comforting in a way that feels pathetic   like clinging to a ritual because the world outside the ritual has stopped making sense.

I stand in front of the mirror in my smallclothes.

I don't know why. I didn't plan to. But my hands are taking stock, running an inventory that feels suddenly urgent, as if I need to record what's here before it changes.

My shoulders. Broad. The muscle dense from years of daily training   not bulky, more like cables wound tight under the skin. I roll them back. They move the way they've always moved.

My chest. Flat. Defined. I can see the ladder of my ribs when I breathe in deep, the valley of my sternum, the faint trail of dark hair that runs from navel to the waistband of my smallclothes.

My arms. Long. Lean. A scar on the left forearm where a hunting knife slipped when I was sixteen. My hands   I hold them up. Large palms, long fingers, calluses on the grip-line from thousands of hours with a sword. These hands have held blades, quills, reins. Have gripped Aldric's arm after a good bout. Have pressed flat against my mother's cheek when she cried and pretended she wasn't.

My jaw. I touch it. Sharp angle. The scratch of stubble that wasn't there this morning. My throat   Adam's apple, prominent, the thing that bobs when I swallow hard.

Below. I'm aware of the weight of my cock, soft against my thigh. Unremarkable. Warm. Mine. I don't touch it   not like that, not tonight. I just... acknowledge it. The way you acknowledge a room you've lived in your whole life when someone tells you you might be moving.

This body has been mine for twenty years. It is the only home I have ever known. It is strong and imperfect and I have never thanked it for anything and I have never had to because it was always going to be here.

Two days.

I get into bed. The sheets are cold, then warm. The fire crackles. Outside, very faintly, I can hear Rowan playing   they must be in the guest quarters overlooking the garden. Something without a melody, just chords, drifting.

I think about my mother's eyes. The fire in them. The certainty. She's already decided. She decided before she called me. The two days aren't for her to make up her mind   they're for me to arrive at the conclusion she's already reached.

I think about Lord Vareth Ashborne. I've met him twice. A large man with a smile that doesn't reach his eyes and hands that grip too hard when he shakes yours. His plans for the kingdom are no secret   taxes doubled, military expansion, the common people pressed into service or poverty. He'd be a king of wolves, and the sheep would bleed.

I think about the anonymous account in blue leather. *Whose are these? And then: mine.*

I think about Rowan's words. *Finding out which parts were real all along.*

I think about the ceiling. Same crack. Same smoke-stain. Same constellation of imperfections I've mapped since I was twelve.

I don't sleep until the hour before dawn and in that single hour, I dream of water   a deep, warm pool, and I'm sinking into it, and it's closing over my head, and I can't tell if I'm drowning or being born.
