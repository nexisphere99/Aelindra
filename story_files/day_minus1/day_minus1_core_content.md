## SCENE 1: QUEEN'S CHAMBERS   ROUND TWO
### [Passage: `day-1_queen`]

I didn't sleep. I should clarify   I slept for one hour, in the grey nothing between deep night and dawn, and dreamed of water closing over my head. So functionally, I didn't sleep. My body moved through the motions of waking anyway: legs off the bed, feet on stone, the hiss through my teeth at the cold. Muscle memory doesn't need consciousness. It just needs a body, and mine   for now   complies.

I dress without looking at the mirror. Not today. The mirror and I have said what we needed to say.

I skip breakfast. My stomach is a clenched fist and the thought of bread and cheese makes something in my throat tighten reflexively. I drink water from the pitcher by the bed. It's been sitting overnight and tastes of iron from the jug and the minerals in the palace well-water. It tastes real. That's enough.

The walk to the east wing is familiar now   I made this walk yesterday and every step felt like a mile. Today it feels like a march. I'm not dragging my feet. I'm advancing. There's a difference. Yesterday I was a man being summoned. Today I'm a man with a list.

I wrote the list at three in the morning by candlelight, because sleep wasn't coming and my mind was a dog chasing its own tail and writing things down was the only way to make the spinning stop. It's on a scrap of parchment in my pocket: every alternative I could think of, every legal angle, every political manoeuvre, every desperate half-baked scheme that came to me in the dark.

The guards bow. I nod. The young soldier from yesterday   the one who looked at me with pity   is gone, replaced by a veteran who looks at me with nothing, which I prefer. The oak door opens.

Mother is awake. She's propped on her pillows, but she's moved to the chaise longue by the window today   a good sign, maybe, or a performance of strength for my benefit. The curtains are half-drawn. Daylight stripes her face: half in light, half in shadow. She looks like a portrait of herself, painted by someone who loved her but couldn't lie about how thin she's become.

"You have a list," she says, before I've said a word. "I can see the parchment in your pocket."

"I have a list."

"Read it."

I pull it out. My hands are steady. I am going to fight this one more time   not because I think I'll win, but because the man I am right now needs to know he tried everything. That when the door finally closes, he was the one who checked every lock.

"Adopt a girl. A commoner with the right blood markers   there must be distant cousins, third or fourth generation, someone "

"The Elder Council requires direct blood descent. 'Blood of the Queen's body,' it reads. No adoption has ever been recognised for succession in six hundred years of case law. Vareth's lawyers   and he has seven, Edrin, seven of the sharpest legal minds in the kingdom, all paid handsomely to find exactly these loopholes and seal them shut   have already filed a preemptive challenge against any adoption claim."

I cross it off. The ink is still wet.

"Then find a bastard cousin. Father surely "

"Your father was faithful to me." She says this without sentiment, as a fact, the way you'd say water is wet. "He was faithful because he loved me and because he lacked the imagination for infidelity. Even if he hadn't been   illegitimate blood is excluded by Clause Seven of the Charter. 'None born outside the sanctified union of the Crown shall bear claim to its inheritance.' The language is explicit. The precedent is unbreakable."

I cross it off.

"Then we change the law. You're the Queen. You've been the Queen for twenty-five years. You have authority "

"I have authority over every law in this kingdom except the Founding Charter. The Charter predates the monarchy   it created the monarchy. Amending it requires a two-thirds vote of the Elder Council. Seven of the twelve Elders are, as of last month, receiving stipends from Ashborne-affiliated trade houses. I've been trying to reform the Council for fifteen years. I was doing it before I was sick, when I had full political capital, when I could call in every favour I was owed. I failed. Fifteen years, Edrin. Do you think you'll succeed in two days?"

My hand tightens on the parchment. The next item. The one I know is desperate.

"Then we dissolve the Elder Council. Declare a state of emergency. Strip their authority "

"That requires a two-thirds vote of the Council itself. The founders   clever bastards that they were   designed it to be un-dissolvable. It's a closed loop. The only body that can dissolve the Council is the Council, and the Council will never vote to dissolve itself."

"Then we FIGHT!" The word comes out louder than I intend. It fills the room, bounces off the stone walls, disturbs the candle flames. Mother doesn't flinch. "We raise the army. Call the banners. Every loyal house "

The cough hits her. Not a polite cough, not a dry tickle   a racking, wet, tearing sound that comes from somewhere deep in her chest and shakes her entire body. She doubles over on the chaise, one hand on her mouth, the other gripping the armrest with knuckles like white bone. It goes on for thirty seconds. Thirty seconds of watching my mother's body try to rip itself apart while I stand three feet away with a list in my hand and nothing useful to do with either.

She pulls the handkerchief away from her mouth. She tries to fold it quickly   hide it   but I see. Red. Not pink, not rust, not the ambiguous brown of old blood. Red. Fresh. The colour of life leaving.

"Vareth has alliances with Velmara, the Iron Reach, and the Coastal Principalities." Her voice is raw now, scraped thin by the cough, but she keeps talking because stopping would be admitting weakness and she will burn to ash before she admits weakness. "Their combined forces outnumber ours three to one. Our generals have war-gamed it. Eight scenarios. We lose all eight. The capital falls in six to eight weeks. The common people   YOUR people, Edrin   die in the thousands. Not soldiers. People. Bakers and farmers and the woman who sells flowers in the lower market and the children who play in the fountain square. They die. Because we chose pride over pragmatism."

The room is silent. The candles gutter. Somewhere in the palace, the clock chimes   counting hours I don't have.

"This isn't a choice." My voice sounds like someone else's. Far away. Hollow.

"No. It's a sacrifice. I'm sorry I'm asking you to make it."

"You're not sorry." And the bitterness in my voice is pure, undiluted, the kind of bitter that comes from knowing someone so well that you can see through their guilt to the calculation beneath. "You'd ask me to walk into fire if the kingdom needed it."

A pause. Her eyes are wet. The fire in them is still there   nothing could extinguish that fire, not illness, not death, not the sight of her only child looking at her with something close to hatred   but the wetness is real. The tears she won't shed are real.

"Yes." Her voice is iron wrapped in velvet. "I would. Because that's what queens do. And that's what I'm asking you to become."

I stand. The parchment with my list is crumpled in my fist. Every item crossed off. Every door closed. Every escape route bricked shut by law, by politics, by military reality, by the simple vicious mathematics of power.

I leave. I don't slam the door. I want to. I want to tear it off its hinges and hurl it down the corridor and watch it splinter against the portraits of my ancestors   all those queens, all those women, six hundred years of them staring down at me from their frames with eyes that say *this is what we did, this is what we were, and now it's your turn.*

Instead I walk. My body shaking   not with fear, not anymore. With rage. At the law. At Vareth. At the founding bastards who wrote the Charter in dragon's blood and made it immutable. At the universe itself, which built this cage with walls of tradition and stone and gave me exactly one key, and the key is shaped like a body that isn't mine.

I reach the end of the corridor. Turn left. The covered bridge to the Moonspire Tower stretches before me   white stone, open on both sides to the wind, the chasm below where the river cuts through rock and the sound of water is the sound of everything falling.

I stop. I'm not going to the tower. I haven't decided. I HAVEN'T DECIDED.

I go to the tower.

## SCENE 2: MOONSPIRE TOWER   MEETING ILARA
### [Passage: `day-1_moonspire`]

The covered bridge is thirty paces long and feels like three hundred.

Below me, the river crashes through the chasm with a sound like the world clearing its throat. The wind funnels through the gap and hits me broadside   cold, sharp, carrying spray that tastes of mineral and deep earth. I grip the stone railing. The stone is ancient, worn smooth by centuries of hands that have crossed this bridge for the same reason: because the tower holds answers that the palace cannot.

The Moonspire Tower rises from the eastern cliff like a single pale bone pointing at the sky. It's white stone   not painted, not plastered, but quarried from something that naturally holds light. Even now, in the grey midday overcast, the tower has a faint luminescence, as if moonlight has been trapped in its walls and leaks out slowly, perpetually, regardless of the hour. It shouldn't be beautiful. It is.

The door is arched, carved with symbols I don't recognise   spiralling forms that could be letters or could be the visual representation of magic itself, frozen in stone. It opens before I touch it. I choose not to find that alarming.

Inside: cool air, a temperature drop that has nothing to do with season and everything to do with whatever enchantments keep this place. The smell is layered   dried herbs, old parchment, beeswax candles, and beneath it all something electric, something that makes the hairs on my forearms stand up and the back of my teeth itch. Magic. I've been around it my whole life   the Silverveil maintain the palace wards, heal the soldiers, advise the crown on matters of the arcane   but I've never been inside their tower. I've never been this close to the source.

Stairs spiral upward, hugging the inner wall. Tapestries hang between sconces   scenes of transformation, not all of them human. A serpent becoming a river. A tree becoming a woman. A bird becoming a song. The images shift slightly when I'm not looking directly at them, as if they're being retold each time the viewer blinks.

I climb. My boots echo on stone that seems to absorb the sound and release it back half a second later, a faint auditory shadow that makes every step feel doubled. One flight. Two. Three. The stairs open onto a landing, and she's there.

Archsorceress Ilara.

She's standing in the doorway of what must be the Ritual Chamber   I can see, beyond her, a round room with a domed ceiling painted in stars and a stone altar at the center, low and wide, draped in silvery cloth. She's framed by the doorway like a portrait, and the effect is clearly intentional. Everything about Ilara feels intentional.

Thirty-eight, according to the palace records. She looks it and she doesn't   her face is sharp-featured, high-cheekboned, the kind of face that will look essentially the same at sixty as it does now because the bones are doing all the work. Dark hair shot through with silver, pulled back from her face and falling to her shoulder blades. Her eyes are the colour of rain on slate   grey with a coldness that isn't unfriendly, just precise. She's wearing the Silverveil robes   deep purple, silver-edged, fitted close to a body that is lean and straight-backed and radiates the particular calm of someone who has seen things that would break most people and chose not to break.

"You're here to ask questions," she says. Her voice is low, measured, the voice of someone who chooses every word and wastes none. "Not to commit."

"How do you know?"

"Because you're standing in the doorway. People who've decided walk in."

I look down. I am, in fact, standing precisely on the threshold   one foot on the landing, one foot on the chamber floor, my body caught between entry and retreat. I step inside. It feels like a concession.

"Sit." She gestures to a chair near a small table where a teapot steams. The tea is poured before I've settled   herbal, faintly sweet, with an undertone of something I can't identify. My first sip and the vice-grip in my chest loosens by a fraction. I look at the cup suspiciously.

"It's lightly enchanted," she says, reading my expression. "Not to influence your decision. To lower your cortisol. You're vibrating like a bowstring and I'd like to have a conversation with a human being, not a panic response."

"I'm not panicking."

"You're breathing twelve times a minute and your jaw is clenched so hard I can hear your teeth grinding. In my professional experience, that qualifies."

I unclench my jaw. Deliberately. She notices, and something that might be amusement crosses her face   not a smile, exactly, but the ghost of one. The shadow a smile casts before the smile itself arrives.

"Ask your questions."

I ask. She answers. Clinically at first   the mechanics of the Rite, the preparation, the procedure, the timeline.

"The ritual takes approximately six hours to perform. You'll drink a preparation   a potion brewed from moonflower essence, silverveil silk, and your own blood. The blood component is what personalises the magic   it reads your body's current template and writes the new one. You'll lie on the altar." She nods toward it. "I and four sister sorceresses will channel transformation magic through your body over those six hours."

"The pain." It's not a question.

"Is significant. Not unbearable   we manage it with enchantments, dampening fields that reduce the sensation to approximately forty percent of its actual intensity. Without the dampening, it would be unsurvivable   the nervous system would shut down in self-preservation. With the dampening, it's... intense. Lord Aldren described it as 'being reforged on an anvil by the gods.' Prince Castien said it was 'the worst night of his life, followed by the first morning of his real one.'"

"That second one sounds like propaganda."

That ghost-smile again. "Castien lived as Princess Casta for forty-seven years. She was beloved. She was powerful. She was, by all accounts, happy. You can decide for yourself whether that's propaganda or outcome data."

"Tell me about the physical changes. Specifically."

She sets down her tea. Her hands fold in her lap   long fingers, silver rings on three of them, nails trimmed short and practical. She speaks the way a surgeon explains a procedure: without hesitation, without euphemism, without apology.

"Your height will decrease by approximately five to six inches. Your skeletal frame will narrow at the shoulders and widen at the pelvis. Your musculature will soften and redistribute   less mass in the upper body, more in the hips and thighs. Your facial bone structure will shift: jawline softens, cheekbones lift, brow ridge smooths, lips become fuller. Your hair will grow   both in length and in density. Your body hair will thin considerably; your facial hair will cease entirely.

"Your skin will change   thinner, softer, with increased sensitivity. Your fat distribution will shift to the breasts, hips, buttocks, and thighs. Your voice will rise   the larynx will reshape, the vocal cords will thin and tighten.

"Your reproductive organs will change completely." She pauses here   not for effect, but to give me a moment. I don't take it. I stare at her and wait. She continues. "Your testes will be absorbed by the body. Your penis will reconfigure into a clitoris and labia   the tissue is homologous, so the magic works with existing structures rather than creating from nothing. A vaginal canal will form, along with a cervix, uterus, and ovaries. You will be capable of menstruation and pregnancy.

"Breast tissue will begin developing during the ritual and will continue growing naturally over the following weeks and months. You'll wake from the Rite at approximately a large A-cup. Growth from there follows a roughly natural timeline, accelerated somewhat by the residual magic."

My tea is cold. I'm not sure when that happened. My hands are wrapped around the cup, gripping it the way you grip something to prove you're still in control of your fingers.

"The transformation is permanent," she says. "There is no reversal. The magic rewrites you at the level of blood and bone. Your chromosomes will change. You will be female   not a man made to resemble a female, not an illusion, not a disguise. Female. Indistinguishable from any woman born as one."

Silence. The candles in the chamber burn without flickering   enchanted flames, steady, unbothered by the chaos in my chest.

"The previous subjects," I manage. "They survived."

"All three."

"And after. Were they... themselves? Still?"

She looks at me. For the first time, something in her expression shifts   the clinical precision softens, just slightly, and I see what might be compassion. Or might be the memory of something she's seen before in this room, in those eyes, in the face of someone sitting where I'm sitting and asking the question I'm asking.

"You will still be you. Your mind, your memories, your personality, your preferences, your humour, your stubbornness"   yes, she says that deliberately   "will remain unchanged. You will think the same thoughts. You will recognise yourself. The body will be different. The person wearing it will be the same."

"She chose it to escape," I say. "Casta. She didn't choose it to be happy."

"No. She chose it to survive. And then she lived. As we all do   making something of what we didn't choose."

I set the cup down. Stand. My legs hold me, which feels like more of an accomplishment than it should.

"I haven't decided."

"The door is always open." She walks me to the landing. At the top of the stairs, she stops. "Until it isn't. The next lunar alignment is tomorrow night. After that, three months. By then "

"By then my mother is dead and Vareth is king."

"Yes."

I descend the stairs. The tapestries shift as I pass   the serpent becoming the river, the tree becoming the woman, the bird becoming the song. The images feel less like illustrations and more like previews.

The bridge is colder on the way back. The river below sounds louder. Everything sounds louder when the world is narrowing to a single point.

## SCENE 3: TRAINING YARD   RAGE SESSION
### [Passage: `day-1_training`]

I don't go to the training yard to train. I go to the training yard to break things.

The practice dummy stands where it always stands   a post wrapped in leather and stuffed with straw, shaped vaguely like a torso, with arms that swing when struck. It's been replaced a hundred times. I've destroyed a handful myself, on bad days. Today is a bad day.

I grab a practice sword from the rack. Not the light one I use for sparring   the heavy one, the one weighted with lead in the pommel that Aldric uses for endurance drills. It's too heavy for finesse. It's perfect for what I need.

The first strike cracks across the dummy's left arm with a sound like a branch snapping. Straw erupts from a split in the leather. Good.

I hit it again. Backhand. The dummy's head whips sideways, wobbles, comes back. I hit it before it's settled. Forehand, overhand, a diagonal strike that catches the torso and gouges a furrow through the leather. Straw bleeds out like intestines from a wound.

I'm not sparring. Sparring requires technique, discipline, the careful architecture of offence and defence that Aldric has spent six years drilling into my muscles. This is the opposite of that. This is demolition. Every swing is the argument I can't win. Every impact is a door closing. The adoption   crack. The bastard claim   crack. The legal reform   crack. The military option   CRACK. The crumpled list in my pocket with every item crossed off   CRACK CRACK CRACK.

The dummy's left arm hangs by a thread. I sever it completely. It falls to the flagstones with a soft thump and lies there, straw leaking, pathetic.

I keep going. The practice sword is heavier with every swing but I don't stop because stopping means thinking and thinking means the Moonspire and Ilara's voice saying *your testes will be absorbed* and *your penis will reconfigure* and *permanent, no reversal, blood and bone* and I cannot think about that right now. I cannot hold those words in my head and remain upright. So I hit things.

Two hours.

I know it's been two hours because the light has changed   the midday overcast has broken and afternoon sun slants across the yard, catching the dust and straw I've launched into the air. My arms burn. The muscles from shoulder to wrist are screaming, not the productive burn of good training but the ragged, tearing protest of tissue pushed past its limits. Blisters have formed on my palms, split open, wept, and crusted. My grip is bloody. The practice sword is bloody. The dummy is gutted   a skeleton of post and nails, straw scattered across ten feet of flagstones like the aftermath of a small, pointless war.

I drop the sword. It clangs on the stone and the sound is very loud in the sudden silence.

I sit. Right there, in the dirt, among the straw and the wreckage. My back against the empty weapon rack. My legs stretched out in front of me. My hands in my lap, palms up   raw, blistered, streaked with blood and straw dust. My breath comes in ragged pulls. My heart is hammering so hard I can feel it in my throat, my wrists, the hollows behind my ears.

These hands. I stare at them. Broad palms. Long fingers. Sword calluses built over six years of daily practice. The scar on the left forearm from the hunting knife. The ridge of tendon that stands up when I flex my wrist. Strong hands. A man's hands.

In forty-eight hours, they could be different hands.

I don't look up when Aldric approaches. I hear him   his boots on the flagstones have a particular cadence, heavy and even, the walk of a man who has never been in a hurry because he's always already where he needs to be. He sits down next to me without being invited. The fact that he's sitting on the ground at all   Aldric, who treats military protocol the way priests treat scripture   tells me he knows this is bad.

He doesn't say anything for a while. We sit in the wreckage together. The afternoon sun warms the stone. A pigeon investigates the scattered straw with the blind optimism of a bird that has never considered the possibility that the world might not provide.

He hands me a waterskin. I drink. The water is tepid and tastes of leather and it's the best thing I've ever put in my mouth.

"I don't know what's troubling you, Your Highness." His voice is careful. Aldric's version of careful   which means direct but gentle, like a sword sheathed in soft leather. "But I've served your mother for twelve years, and I know that look. It's the look of someone being asked to carry something too heavy."

"What if the weight is... everything I am?"

He considers this. Aldric considers things the way he fights   thoroughly, without rushing, with the understanding that a wrong response is worse than a slow one.

"Then you carry it until your legs learn it. That's all any of us do."

Simple. Aldric is always simple   not because he's stupid, but because he's stripped away everything that isn't essential. He sees the world as a series of duties, loyalties, and the spaces between them. He doesn't know what I'm considering. He doesn't need to. He sees a man in pain, and he sits beside him, and he offers water.

It helps. Not enough. But it helps.

He stands first. Offers his hand. I take it   his grip is warm and calloused and strong and absolutely, utterly certain. He pulls me up.

"I'll have someone replace the dummy," he says, looking at the wreckage with the expression of a man surveying a battlefield.

"I think I owe the armoury an apology."

"You owe the armoury three dummies. I'll add it to the requisition." The ghost of something warm in his voice. "Get some food. And wrap those hands   the blisters will get infected if you leave them."

He walks away. His back is straight, his stride is even, and watching him cross the training yard I think: *that man would follow me into fire. And I'm about to become something he may not recognise.*

## SCENE 4: EDRIN'S CHAMBERS   TELLING ROWAN
### [Passage: `day-1_rowan`]

Evening. The fire is lit   someone came in while I was destroying training dummies, rebuilt it, left fresh candles and a tray of food I haven't touched. My hands are wrapped in linen bandages that I did myself, badly. The blisters sting beneath.

The knock is Rowan's knock   three taps, a pause, two more, our old signal from years of late-night conversations in tournament camps and borrowed rooms in taverns where the walls were thin and the innkeepers were nosy.

"It's open."

They come in carrying wine   a clay jug, unstoppered, the kind you buy from the kitchen stores rather than the royal cellar because Rowan has never had the patience for formality. And cheese. A wedge of something sharp and crumbly, wrapped in cloth. And their lute, slung across their back because Rowan without a lute is like a knight without a sword   technically functional but fundamentally incomplete.

"You look like you lost a fight with a threshing machine," they say, dropping to the floor in front of the fire because Rowan has never, in the entirety of our friendship, sat in a chair when a floor was available. They claim furniture creates artificial hierarchy. I think they just like being on the ground.

"Training dummy."

"Did the dummy win?"

"The dummy is in several pieces."

"Then you won. Congratulations. You've defeated practice straw." They pour wine into two cups   mismatched, one ceramic, one wooden, both stolen from the kitchen. "Drink."

I sit on the floor across from them. The fire is warm at my back. The wine is rough, dark, slightly sour   tavern wine, not court wine. It doesn't pretend to be anything. I drink it and the warmth spreads down my throat and into my chest and loosens something that's been locked tight since this morning.

We sit. We drink. Rowan picks at the cheese and eats the rind first, which is a habit I've never understood and they've never explained. The fire crackles. Outside, through the arrow-slit window, the sky is turning the deep bruised purple of early evening, and the first stars are showing, and the world looks exactly the way it's always looked, which feels like a lie.

"I need to tell you something."

Rowan sets down their cup. They don't say *what is it* or *are you alright* or any of the reflexive responses that most people offer when you announce bad news. They just look at me. Their honey-coloured eyes are steady. They wait.

I tell them. Everything.

The succession crisis. The Elder Council. Vareth's alliances, his lawyers, his army, his seven legal minds paid to seal every loophole shut. Mother's illness   worse than anyone outside the east wing knows, the blood in the handkerchief she doesn't hide quickly enough, the cough that racks her body like something inside is trying to claw its way out. The Founding Charter, unbreakable, un-amendable, a six-hundred-year-old cage. The list in my pocket, every item crossed off.

And the Rite. The Moonspire. Ilara. The altar. The potion. The six hours of pain. The permanent, irreversible, total transformation. Bones reshaping. Height dropping. Organs... changing. Everything that I am, physically, replaced by something else. Something female. Something that can sit on the throne and bear an heir and satisfy the bloodless bureaucratic language of a law written by dead men in an age when dragons may or may not have been real.

I tell them about the restricted diary in the library   *whose are these?* I tell them about Ilara's explanation   clinical, precise, devastating. I tell them about the training dummy, the two hours of hitting things because the alternative was screaming.

I tell them things I haven't told anyone. That I'm terrified. That I feel like I'm being erased   not killed, which would be simpler and in some horrible way more dignified, but erased. Rewritten. The person who is me, overwritten by a person who is also me but lives in a body I can't imagine and didn't ask for. That every time I look at my own hands now, I see something temporary. A draft. A rough sketch that's about to be painted over.

Rowan listens without interrupting. Not because they're patient   Rowan is many things but naturally patient is not one of them   but because they understand that this isn't a conversation. It's an exorcism. I'm casting out every fear by naming it, and the naming requires silence from everyone except the person doing the naming.

When I finish, my voice is hoarse. The wine is half gone. The fire has burned lower. Rowan is quiet for a long time.

"You want me to tell you not to do it."

"I want SOMEONE to tell me there's another way."

"There isn't." Their voice is gentle. Rowan's gentleness is different from Aldric's   Aldric is gentle the way a gloved hand is soft, padding over steel. Rowan is gentle the way water is gentle   shapeless, adaptable, finding every crack. "I know you. You've already looked for every other door. You wouldn't be telling me if you hadn't exhausted them all."

"So you think I should."

"I think you will. Because you love your mother, and you love this kingdom, and you love the people who live in it more than you love the shape of your own jaw."

The words land. They land because they're true and because Rowan is the only person alive who could say them without it sounding like a judgement. From anyone else, it would sound like *you should sacrifice yourself.* From Rowan, it sounds like *I see who you are, and the person I see would not let others burn.*

Something breaks in me. Not dramatically   not a collapse, not a scene from a mummer's play. Just a crack. A fissure in the wall I've been holding up since yesterday morning, since the oak door and the blood-smell and the grey-blue eyes and *by becoming the heir this kingdom needs.*

"I'm not ready " My voice cracks. Actually cracks, the way it hasn't since I was fourteen and still growing and my body couldn't decide what register to speak in.

Rowan moves. They set their cup down, shift across the floor to my side, and put an arm around my shoulders. Their body is warm. Their lute-calloused fingers grip my upper arm with a strength that surprises me   Rowan looks delicate, built for music and movement, but the grip is real and solid and present.

"No one's ever ready to become someone new," they say, their voice close to my ear, breath warm. "You just... wake up one morning and realize you already did."

I lean into them. Just for a moment. Just because there's no one else in the world I can be this with   this small, this scared, this unmade. My forehead against their shoulder. The leather of their vest smells like road-dust and campfire smoke and the faintest trace of rosin from their lute strings. It smells like every summer I've spent counting the days until they visit.

"Will you still..." I can't finish. The words stick.

"Will I still what? Know you? See you? Be here?"

"When I'm..."

"I'll still be here. You'll still be you. Just... more." They squeeze my arm. "Edrin. Listen.
